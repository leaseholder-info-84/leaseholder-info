Leaseholder Rights

A plain-English guide to questions leaseholders can ask, the legal framework in England, and the appropriate routes for information, complaint, redress and tribunal action.

Important: This is general information for leaseholders in England, not legal advice. Always check your lease, company articles, specific statutory wording, and official guidance before relying on a right or starting legal proceedings.

1. Who is the Principal Accountable Person (PAP) for my building?

What it is

For an occupied higher-risk building, the Principal Accountable Person (PAP) is the Accountable Person responsible for the structure and exterior of the building. In practical terms, this will often be the freeholder, a resident management company, a right-to-manage company, or another organisation holding the relevant legal estate.

The fact to establish

Ask for the PAP's legal name, company number, registered address, named point of contact and the building's BSR registration details. For registered higher-risk buildings, the public register is a useful independent starting point.

Law and legislation: Building Safety Act 2022, sections 72–77; Higher-Risk Buildings (Descriptions and Supplementary Provisions) Regulations 2023.
Who to contact: The Principal Accountable Person; the managing agent; the BSR public register; HM Land Registry for title information.
2. What resident-engagement strategy must be in place?

What it is

The PAP of an occupied higher-risk building must prepare a written Residents' Engagement Strategy, consult residents and owners on it, and act in accordance with it.

The fact to establish

Ask for the current strategy, the date it was last reviewed, evidence of consultation, the communication methods used, how responses are recorded, and how the PAP will explain decisions that affect building-safety risks.

Law and legislation: Building Safety Act 2022, section 91; Higher-Risk Buildings (Management of Safety Risks etc.) (England) Regulations 2023, Part 2.
Who to contact: The PAP first. If the building-safety complaints process has been exhausted, report relevant concerns to the Building Safety Regulator.
3. What building-safety information must I receive automatically?

What it is

Accountable Persons for occupied higher-risk buildings have duties to provide prescribed building-safety information proactively to residents and owners of residential units.

The fact to establish

Ask what information has been issued, when it was issued, the format used, and how it covers safety risks, evacuation information, the identity and contact details of the relevant dutyholders, and routes for reporting concerns.

Law and legislation: Building Safety Act 2022, section 89; Higher-Risk Buildings (Keeping and Provision of Information etc.) (England) Regulations 2024, especially regulation 7 and the prescribed-information schedules.
Who to contact: The PAP or Accountable Person. Retain a dated copy of any request and response.
4. What building-safety documents can I request?

What it is

A resident aged 16 or over, and an owner of a residential unit, may request prescribed information or documents from the Accountable Person for an occupied higher-risk building.

The fact to establish

Make a specific written request. Depending on the request and applicable exceptions, this may include documents or information relating to the safety case, safety risks, risk assessments, inspection arrangements, resident engagement and the building-safety management system.

Law and legislation: Building Safety Act 2022, section 92; Higher-Risk Buildings (Keeping and Provision of Information etc.) (England) Regulations 2024, including regulations 14–18 and Schedule 3.
Who to contact: The PAP or relevant Accountable Person. If information is withheld, ask for the statutory basis and whether an exception—for example, security—has been relied on.
5. What complaint process must exist for building-safety concerns?

What it is

The PAP must establish and operate a system for investigating relevant building-safety complaints. It should be accessible and tell residents how concerns will be received, investigated and answered.

The fact to establish

Ask for the current written procedure, contact address, acknowledgement and response targets, escalation route, and confirmation that your complaint has been logged as a building-safety complaint.

Law and legislation: Building Safety Act 2022, section 93; Higher-Risk Buildings (Management of Safety Risks etc.) (England) Regulations 2023.
Who to contact: The PAP's designated complaint contact first. When that procedure has been exhausted, see BSR guidance on building-safety complaints.
6. What happens if there is a serious building-safety occurrence?

What it is

Accountable Persons have a mandatory-occurrence reporting regime for certain safety occurrences that create, or risk creating, a significant risk to the safety of people in or about a higher-risk building.

The fact to establish

Ask whether an incident has been assessed against the reportable-occurrence criteria, who made that assessment, what immediate risk controls were applied and whether a report was made to the BSR where required.

Law and legislation: Building Safety Act 2022, section 87; Higher-Risk Buildings (Mandatory Occurrence Reporting) (England) Regulations 2023.
Who to contact: Report an immediate threat to life through 999. For building-safety management concerns, use the PAP's process and, after that route is exhausted, the BSR.
7. Was proper Section 20 consultation carried out for major works?

What it is

Section 20 consultation protects leaseholders where qualifying works would result in any leaseholder contributing more than £250, or where a qualifying long-term agreement would cost any leaseholder more than £100 in any accounting period.

What a qualifying-works consultation should look like

  1. Notice of Intention: A written notice describing the proposed works, explaining why they are necessary, inviting observations and allowing contractor nominations. The consultation period must be at least 30 days.
  2. Notice of Estimates: A written notice made after estimates are obtained, explaining where estimates can be inspected, setting out estimates as required by the Regulations, responding to relevant observations and allowing a further consultation period of at least 30 days.
  3. Notice of Award: Required in specified circumstances after contract award, including where the chosen contractor is not the lowest estimate or not a contractor nominated by leaseholders. It must state the reasons for the award and information about observations.

The fact to establish

Ask for each notice, the date and method of service, the complete specification, every estimate, leaseholder observations and responses, contractor nominations, the selection rationale, contract award date, and evidence that no commitment was made prematurely.

Law and legislation: Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, sections 20 and 20ZA; Service Charges (Consultation Requirements) (England) Regulations 2003.
Who to contact: The landlord or managing agent first. For disputed payability, reasonableness or consultation compliance, consider the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber).
8. What if Section 20 consultation was not done properly?

What it is

Failure to comply with Section 20 limits recovery from an individual leaseholder to £250 for qualifying works, or £100 in an accounting period for a qualifying long-term agreement, unless the First-tier Tribunal grants dispensation.

The fact to establish

Ask whether a dispensation application has been made, obtain the application and evidence, and identify the practical prejudice caused by the lack of consultation: for example, lost opportunity to comment on scope, nominate a contractor, challenge price, or influence timing and procurement.

Law and legislation: Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, section 20ZA; Service Charges (Consultation Requirements) (England) Regulations 2003. The leading case is Daejan Investments Ltd v Benson [2013] UKSC 14, which addresses prejudice and dispensation.
Who to contact: First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber); LEASE for independent leasehold information.
9. How do I challenge an unreasonable or non-payable service charge?

What it is

The First-tier Tribunal can determine whether a service charge is payable and, if so, who must pay it, how much is payable, when it is payable and how it may be paid.

The fact to establish

Identify the exact demand and the relevant lease clauses. Gather the demand, budget, accounts, invoices, consultation documents, correspondence, specifications and evidence showing why the cost was not reasonably incurred, the work was not of a reasonable standard, the demand is not permitted by the lease, or the sum is otherwise not payable.

Law and legislation: Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, section 19 (reasonableness) and section 27A (Tribunal determination of payability).
Who to contact: The landlord or managing agent first; then the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber) using the current HMCTS leasehold application process.
10. How do I inspect service-charge accounts, invoices and receipts?

What it is

Leaseholders can request a written summary of relevant service-charge costs. After receiving it, they can require reasonable facilities to inspect accounts, receipts and other supporting documents and take copies or extracts.

The fact to establish

Make the request in writing, quote the relevant statutory section, retain proof of delivery and request a clear appointment for inspection. Ask to see invoices, receipts, contracts, tender material, insurance documents, bank or trust-account evidence where relevant, and the allocation calculation for your flat.

Law and legislation: Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, sections 21 and 22. Time limits and criminal offences for non-compliance are set out in those provisions.
Who to contact: The landlord, management company or managing agent named in the service-charge demand; LEASE for guidance; the First-tier Tribunal for relevant payability disputes.
11. Can I pay a disputed service charge under protest and still challenge it?

What it is

Yes. A leaseholder can pay while disputing the charge and still apply to the First-tier Tribunal for a determination. Payment alone does not mean that the leaseholder has agreed or admitted the charge.

The fact to establish

Paying may reduce the risk of arrears, recovery action and potential forfeiture consequences, but it does not itself decide the dispute. If you pay, write at the same time to identify the demand, state that payment is made without admission of liability and under protest, reserve your rights, and request the evidence needed to assess the charge.

Example reservation of rights:
“I am paying £[amount] in respect of the service-charge demand dated [date]. This payment is made under protest and without admission that the sum is properly payable under my lease. I reserve all rights to seek a determination from the First-tier Tribunal under section 27A of the Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, including recovery of any amount found not to be payable. Please provide the complete supporting documents for the disputed items.”
Law and legislation: Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, section 27A(2): the Tribunal's jurisdiction applies whether or not payment has been made. Section 27A(3): a tenant is not taken to have agreed or admitted a matter merely because payment has been made.
Who to contact: Send the written reservation to the landlord or managing agent when paying. For a ruling, apply to the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber). Consider independent legal advice before withholding substantial sums.
12. Am I protected from historical building-safety defect costs?

What it is

Building Safety Act leaseholder protections can restrict or prevent service-charge recovery for remediation of relevant historical building-safety defects, subject to statutory definitions, building eligibility, landlord status and leaseholder qualification.

The fact to establish

Do not assume that a label such as “building safety” decides liability. Ask what defect is alleged, when it arose, why it is a relevant defect, whether the work is remediation, who the accountable landlord is, whether a Landlord Certificate has been issued, and how qualifying-lease protections have been assessed.

Law and legislation: Building Safety Act 2022, Part 5 and Schedule 8; Building Safety (Leaseholder Protections) (Information etc.) (England) Regulations 2022.
Who to contact: The landlord or freeholder for Landlord Certificate information; LEASE; specialist legal advice where significant remediation costs are proposed; the First-tier Tribunal where appropriate.
13. What are the responsibilities of a Director of a Residents' Management Company (RMC)?

What it is

Directors of a resident management company (RMC) or right-to-manage (RTM) company are officers of a corporate entity. Even as unpaid leaseholder volunteers, they are bound by strict statutory duties codified in the Companies Act 2006. Directors owe their legal duties to the company as a whole, meaning they must act lawfully, impartially, and in accordance with the company's constitution.

The seven statutory director duties

  1. Act within powers (s.171): Comply with the company's Articles of Association and only exercise powers for the purposes for which they were conferred.
  2. Promote the success of the company (s.172): Act in good faith to promote the success of the company for the benefit of its members as a whole, taking into account long-term consequences and the need to act fairly between members.
  3. Exercise independent judgment (s.173): Form honest, independent decisions rather than blindly deferring to managing agents or individual board members.
  4. Exercise reasonable care, skill and diligence (s.174): Act with the care, skill and diligence expected of a reasonably diligent person with the general knowledge and skill that the director actually possesses.
  5. Avoid conflicts of interest (s.175): Avoid situations where personal or commercial interests clash with the company's interests.
  6. Not accept benefits from third parties (s.176): Never accept gifts, commissions, or hospitality from contractors, insurers, or managing agents.
  7. Declare interests in transactions or arrangements (s.177): Fully disclose any direct or indirect interest in any proposed contract or arrangement before the company enters into it.

The fact to establish

Ask for the company's Articles of Association, board minutes, signed conflict-of-interest declarations, and the professional advice the board relied on when making high-value financial or procurement decisions. Check whether directors have complied with statutory filing duties at Companies House (annual accounts and confirmation statements).

Law and legislation: Companies Act 2006, sections 171–177 (General duties of directors) and sections 303–305 (Members' statutory right to requisition a general meeting by 5% of voting rights).
Who to contact: The Company Secretary / Registered Office of the RMC; Companies House for public company filings; LEASE; or specialist corporate solicitors if a board acts ultra vires or in breach of statutory duty.
14. What is a Recognised Tenants' Association (RTA) and why does it matter?

What it is

A Recognised Tenants' Association (RTA) is an independent, democratic association of leaseholders who contribute to common service charges that has been formally recognised either by written agreement from the landlord or by a certificate from the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber). Once recognised, an RTA holds powerful collective statutory rights that individual leaseholders do not possess alone.

Statutory rights of an RTA

  • Section 20 consultation nominations: During major works consultations, an RTA has the statutory right to nominate a contractor from whom the landlord must try to obtain an estimate.
  • Right to appoint a surveyor: Under the Housing Act 1996, an RTA can appoint a qualified surveyor to inspect documents, common parts, and advise on service charges.
  • Right to be consulted on managing agents: The landlord must consult the RTA on the appointment or re-appointment of a managing agent.
  • Service-charge scrutiny: An RTA secretary can request statutory summaries of service-charge accounts and inspect underlying vouchers on behalf of all members.

The fact to establish

To obtain tribunal recognition in England, the association must generally represent at least 50% of the qualifying tenants, have an open constitution, democratic elections (Chair, Secretary, Treasurer), clear voting rules, and full independence from the landlord or developer.

Law and legislation: Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, section 29; The Tenants' Associations (Provisions Relating to Recognition and Provision of Information) (England) Regulations 2018 (SI 2018/1043); Housing Act 1996, section 84 and Schedule 4.
Who to contact: Apply in writing to the landlord for formal recognition. If refused or ignored, apply to the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber) for an RTA recognition certificate.
15. What responsibilities should a paid managing agent perform?

What it is

A paid managing agent is a professional contractor appointed by the landlord, RMC, or RTM company to carry out day-to-day estate administration under a formal management agreement. While the landlord or RMC remains ultimately legally responsible, the agent is paid management fees from the service charge and must perform their duties in accordance with statutory requirements, contract terms, and professional standards.

Core professional responsibilities

  1. Statutory accounting and trust accounts: Hold all service-charge funds in designated statutory trust accounts (s.42 Landlord and Tenant Act 1987), produce clear annual budgets, reconcile year-end accounts promptly, and issue compliant statutory demands with required summary notices.
  2. Consultation compliance: Strictly administer Section 20 consultation processes for any qualifying works exceeding £250 per unit or contracts exceeding £100 per unit per year before instructing work or billing levies.
  3. Building maintenance and procurement: Commission proactive inspections, obtain competitive quotes, ensure contractors carry valid public liability insurance, and monitor quality of workmanship on communal repairs.
  4. Statutory health and safety: Organise and log required periodic assessments (Fire Risk Assessments, legionella testing, lift safety, asbestos surveys, electrical inspections) and assist dutyholders with Higher-Risk Building obligations where instructed.
  5. Transparent communication: Maintain accessible complaint-handling systems, respond within reasonable timelines, and give leaseholders access to invoices and vouchers under sections 21 and 22.

The fact to establish

Ask for a copy of the written Management Agreement (specifying fee structure, core services versus additional fee items), proof of designated trust accounts for client monies, proof of professional indemnity insurance, and current membership details for a government-approved redress scheme.

Law and legislation: Landlord and Tenant Act 1985 (ss.18–30); Landlord and Tenant Act 1987 (s.42 trust fund requirements); RICS Service Charge Residential Management Code (statutory approved code under s.87 Leasehold Reform, Housing and Urban Development Act 1993).
Who to contact: The designated senior property manager or director of the managing agent; the RMC/Landlord client; The Property Ombudsman or Property Redress Scheme.
16. What complaint process exists for my managing agent?

What it is

A property-management agent carrying out qualifying property-management work in England must belong to an approved redress scheme. The agent should publish or supply a written complaints-handling procedure and identify its redress-scheme membership.

The fact to establish

Ask for the agent's written complaints procedure, named complaint handler, stage-one and final-response deadlines, redress scheme membership number, and whether the issue concerns the agent's conduct, the landlord's decision, or a lease/legal dispute. These are not always the same matter and can require different routes.

Typical escalation route

  1. Raise a clear written complaint with the managing agent, setting out the facts, dates, documents, remedy sought and deadline for response.
  2. Complete the agent's internal complaints procedure and obtain a final viewpoint letter, or wait for the scheme's required elapsed period where no response is given.
  3. Escalate to the agent's actual redress scheme: either The Property Ombudsman (TPO) or the Property Redress Scheme (PRS).
Law and legislation: Enterprise and Regulatory Reform Act 2013, section 83; Redress Schemes for Lettings Agency Work and Property Management Work (Requirement to Join etc.) (England) Order 2014.
Who to contact: The managing agent; The Property Ombudsman; Property Redress Scheme. Check which scheme the agent actually belongs to before escalating.
17. Can leaseholders take over management from the landlord?

What it is

The Right to Manage (RTM) allows qualifying leaseholders to take over management functions through an RTM company without having to prove fault by the landlord. Collective enfranchisement is a separate route through which qualifying leaseholders may seek to acquire the freehold.

The fact to establish

Assess the building's eligibility, the number of qualifying tenants, participation levels, commercial-use limits, existing management arrangements, and the precise functions that would transfer. RTM is procedural: notice requirements, company structure and statutory deadlines matter.

Law and legislation: Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Act 2002, Part 2 (Right to Manage); Leasehold Reform, Housing and Urban Development Act 1993 (collective enfranchisement).
Who to contact: LEASE; an RTM specialist; a solicitor and valuer experienced in collective enfranchisement where freehold acquisition is being considered.
18. What types of leasehold dispute can the First-tier Tribunal decide?

What it is

The First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber) is the specialist tribunal for many residential leasehold disputes in England. It is not a general complaints service: its jurisdiction depends on the particular statute and remedy sought.

Common application routes

  • Service-charge payability and reasonableness: Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, section 27A.
  • Dispensation from consultation requirements: Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, section 20ZA.
  • Appointment of a manager: Landlord and Tenant Act 1987, section 24, where statutory grounds and notice requirements are met.
  • Administration charges: Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Act 2002, Schedule 11.
  • Right to Manage disputes: Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Act 2002, Part 2.
  • Building-safety remedies: Building Safety Act 2022 provisions including remediation orders and remediation contribution orders, where applicable.

The fact to establish

Before applying, identify the correct legal basis, respondent, remedy, disputed figures, lease clauses and evidence. Preserve a dated chronology and include only documents that support the issues the Tribunal has power to decide.

Law and legislation: Tribunals, Courts and Enforcement Act 2007; Landlord and Tenant Act 1985; Landlord and Tenant Act 1987; Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Act 2002; Building Safety Act 2022.
Who to contact: HM Courts & Tribunals Service — First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber); LEASE; a solicitor or barrister for high-value, urgent or legally complex cases.
