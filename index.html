Leaseholder Information

A general educational resource for leaseholders in England. Use this page for context and questions to investigate; do not rely on it alone or take financial, legal, or other consequential action without checking the current legislation, your lease, official guidance, and—where appropriate—independent legal advice.

Important: This page gives general legal information for leaseholders in England. It is not legal advice, does not determine liability for any specific building, person, company, service-charge demand or work, and should always be read alongside the lease, company constitution, full statutory text, current regulations, relevant evidence and official guidance. Information can change, and examples are general rather than findings about any particular person, company, building, or dispute.

Part 1: General Leasehold Rights, Consultation & Block Governance

Applies to residential leasehold properties across England, covering service charges, consultation, company directors, managing agents, and the Tribunal.

Was proper Section 20 consultation carried out for major works?

What it is

Section 20 consultation requirements may apply where qualifying works would result in any one leaseholder contributing more than £250, or where a qualifying long-term agreement would require any one leaseholder to contribute more than £100 in an accounting year, subject to the statutory definitions, exceptions and the lease.

What a qualifying-works consultation should look like

  1. Notice of Intention: A written notice describing the proposed works, explaining why they are necessary, inviting written observations and allowing leaseholder contractor nominations. The consultation period must run for at least 30 days.
  2. Notice of Estimates: A written notice prepared after tenders are received. It must make at least two estimates available for inspection (including any estimate from a leaseholder-nominated contractor), summarise previous observations, provide cost breakdowns, and allow a further 30-day consultation window.
  3. Notice of Award: Required where the awarded contractor was neither the lowest tender nor a leaseholder nominee. It must explain the specific reasons for the award and summarise observations within 21 days of entering into the contract.

The fact to establish

Ask for copies of all served notices, exact dates and methods of service, specifications, tenders received, leaseholder nominations, and proof that no contractor was appointed or work started before the process lawfully concluded.

Law and legislation: Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, sections 20 and 20ZA; Service Charges (Consultation Requirements) (England) Regulations 2003 (SI 2003/1987).
Who to contact: The landlord or managing agent first. If consultation was breached, consider applying to the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber).
What is a sinking or reserve fund, how is it funded, what is it used for, and does Section 20 still apply?

What it is

A sinking fund, also called a reserve fund, is money collected in advance from leaseholders through the service charge to help meet the cost of future major expenditure, such as roof replacement, external repairs, lift renewal, or other substantial cyclical works, where the lease permits this type of fund.

Although it is often described as a separate pot, it is still service-charge money collected in advance and held for the contributing leaseholders.

How it is funded

A reserve fund is usually funded by regular contributions demanded under the lease as part of the service charge. The lease should identify whether reserve or sinking-fund contributions can be collected, what they can be used for, and how the contributions are apportioned between leaseholders.

What it is used for

A reserve fund is generally intended to spread the cost of irregular but foreseeable major expenditure over time, rather than forcing leaseholders to meet the full cost in one year. Typical examples may include major roof works, structural repairs, external decoration, lift replacement, or renewal of communal plant, subject to the wording of the lease.

It should not simply be assumed that reserve money can be used for any item of expenditure. The fact to establish is whether the lease authorises the fund, what categories of work it covers, and whether the proposed use falls within that wording.

Does Section 20 still apply?

Yes. Section 20 consultation is not avoided simply because the landlord or management company proposes to pay for works from the reserve or sinking fund rather than by issuing a new levy. If the works are qualifying works and the contribution attributable to any one leaseholder exceeds £250, the statutory consultation requirements normally still apply.

The key distinction is that the sinking fund describes the source of the money, whereas Section 20 concerns the legal consultation process that may be required before the cost is incurred and recovered through the service charge machinery.

The fact to establish

Ask to see the lease wording that authorises reserve-fund contributions, the accounting records showing how the fund is held, and the Section 20 consultation documents for any major works said to be payable from that fund. If consultation was not carried out, ask what statutory basis is relied on for that position and whether Tribunal dispensation was obtained.

Law and legislation: Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, sections 18, 19, 20 and 20ZA; Service Charges (Consultation Requirements) (England) Regulations 2003; Landlord and Tenant Act 1987, section 42; LEASE guidance on reserve funds and Section 20 consultation.
Who to contact: The landlord, Resident Management Company, or managing agent first. If documents are withheld, use the statutory service-charge information rights. If the dispute concerns payability, reasonableness, or consultation compliance, consider the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber).
What does a valid, unbiased, and lawful Section 20 consultation look like?

What it is

Consultation under Section 20 is not a superficial "tick-box" exercise or an announcement of decisions already taken. In law, consultation must be genuine, open-minded, and conducted when proposals are still at a formative stage. The landlord or managing agent must approach the process with a willingness to be persuaded by leaseholder observations and nominations.

Core legal hallmarks of a valid, unbiased consultation

  1. Conducted at a formative stage: The consultation notices must be served before any contract is awarded, work is instructed, purchase order is signed, or binding commitment is made.
  2. Objective and coherent statement of reasons: The landlord must state the genuine, underlying reason why works are required (supported by defect surveys or independent reports). Citing irrelevant reports or unrelated projects invalidates the clarity needed for leaseholders to respond.
  3. Independent and open tendering: The landlord must make a bona fide effort to obtain an estimate from any contractor nominated by a leaseholder or Recognised Tenants' Association (RTA), and evaluate all tenders against consistent specifications without pre-favouring an incumbent contractor.
  4. Conscientious consideration of observations: The landlord owes a mandatory statutory duty to have genuine regard to written observations. They must provide individual or summarised written responses addressing the substance of leaseholder queries, not dismissive rejections.
  5. Unbundled, transparent specifications: Separate, distinct projects (e.g. roof repairs vs drainage vs communal doors) should not be artificially bundled into a single confused notice to evade scrutiny or restrict competitive bidding.
Valid, Unbiased Consultation Defective or Predetermined Consultation
Proposals consulted on before appointing a contractor Contractor already chosen or instructed before notices expire
Leaseholder-nominated contractors invited to quote on identical terms Nominated contractors ignored or subjected to unreasonable hurdles
Clear, detailed specifications showing locations and scopes Vague descriptions or cut-and-paste text from unrelated reports
Full estimates and line-item breakdowns available to inspect free Only lump sums revealed; refusal to provide tender specifications
Statutory 30-day windows strictly observed before taking action Levies demanded on short 7-day deadlines during consultation

The fact to establish

Ask for the complete consultation audit trail: the exact tender documents sent to all contractors, proof that nominated contractors were contacted, the date-stamped tender returns, and the written record of how the board considered and responded to every leaseholder observation.

Law and legislation: Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, section 20; Service Charges (Consultation Requirements) (England) Regulations 2003; R v Secretary of State for Social Services ex p Association of Metropolitan Authorities [1986] 1 WLR 1 (the Sedley requirements for lawful consultation); Daejan Investments Ltd v Benson [2013] UKSC 14.
Who to contact: Landlord, Resident Management Company, or managing agent; LEASE; or apply to the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber) to challenge consultation validity.
What if Section 20 consultation was not done properly?

What it is

If a landlord or management company fails to comply with statutory Section 20 consultation requirements, the amount that may be recoverable from an individual leaseholder may be limited to the applicable statutory threshold unless the First-tier Tribunal grants dispensation, subject to the precise statutory requirements, the lease and the facts.

The fact to establish

Ask whether an application for dispensation under Section 20ZA has been submitted to the First-tier Tribunal. Leaseholders can oppose dispensation by identifying concrete prejudice: for example, being denied the ability to propose alternative contractors, dispute unreasonable specifications, or test market pricing.

Law and legislation: Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, section 20ZA. The leading Supreme Court authority is Daejan Investments Ltd v Benson [2013] UKSC 14.
Who to contact: The First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber); LEASE for free procedural advice.
How do I challenge an unreasonable or non-payable service charge?

What it is

A leaseholder may be able to ask the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber) to determine whether a service charge is payable and whether the relevant costs were reasonably incurred, subject to the Tribunal’s jurisdiction, the lease and any applicable procedural limitation.

The fact to establish

Cross-reference demands against lease provisions. The Tribunal examines: (1) whether the lease permits the landlord to recharge the item, (2) whether costs were reasonably incurred, and (3) whether works or services were provided to a reasonable standard.

Law and legislation: Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, section 19 (reasonableness test) and section 27A (Tribunal jurisdiction to determine payability).
Who to contact: Landlord or managing agent via formal written dispute; then apply to the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber) using form LEASE1.
How do I inspect service-charge accounts, invoices and receipts?

What it is

Leaseholders may have statutory rights to request a summary of relevant service-charge costs and, after obtaining the relevant summary, to request reasonable facilities for inspecting supporting accounts, receipts and other documents, subject to the statutory conditions and timescales.

The fact to establish

A written section 21 request may be used to request a summary of relevant costs. After obtaining the relevant summary, a leaseholder may make a written section 22 request within the statutory period for reasonable facilities to inspect the accounts, receipts and other supporting documents. The statutory response and inspection periods should be checked against the current legislation.

Law and legislation: Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, sections 21 and 22. Unjustified failure to comply without reasonable excuse is a statutory summary offence under section 25.
Who to contact: Landlord, Resident Management Company, or managing agent; your local housing authority (which has powers to prosecute Section 25 offences); or the First-tier Tribunal.
Can I pay a disputed service charge under protest and still challenge it?

What it is

Payment of a disputed service charge does not necessarily prevent a later challenge, but the effect of payment depends on the statutory provision, the lease, the wording used and the circumstances. A contemporaneous reservation of rights may help preserve the position, but it does not guarantee protection against recovery, forfeiture or other enforcement action.

The fact to establish

Pay the sum alongside a contemporaneous written reservation of rights expressly stating that payment is made without admission that the certified sum is lawfully payable.

Example reservation of rights:
“I am paying the sum of £[amount] in respect of demand [invoice number] dated [date]. This payment is made strictly under protest and without admission of liability that the charges are properly due or reasonable under the lease covenants. I reserve all statutory rights to challenge the payability and reasonableness of this charge at the First-tier Tribunal under section 27A of the Landlord and Tenant Act 1985 and to seek a full refund. Please supply supporting vouchers and tenders for this expenditure.”
Law and legislation: Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, section 27A(2) (jurisdiction applies whether or not payment has been made) and section 27A(3) (payment does not constitute agreement or admission). See also Sara & Hossein Asset Holdings v Blacks Outdoor Retail Ltd [2023] UKSC 2.
Who to contact: Landlord or managing agent; the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber).
Am I protected from historical building-safety defect costs? (And does the 27 June 2022 cutoff limit current works?)

What it is

Schedule 8 of the Building Safety Act 2022 may restrict, cap or eliminate the amount recoverable from qualifying leaseholders for certain relevant defects, subject to the statutory definitions, qualifying-lease conditions, landlord and building circumstances, certification requirements and any applicable regulations.

Does the 27 June 2022 date mean current works are unprotected?

No. There is common confusion regarding the statutory date of 27 June 2022:

  • When the defect was created (1992–2022): Under section 120 of the Act, a "relevant defect" is a fault arising from construction, refurbishment, or conversion work completed in the 30-year retrospective window between 28 June 1992 and 27 June 2022.
  • When the remediation work is done (Present and Future): Remediation carried out now or in the future may fall within the statutory leaseholder protections where it relates to a relevant defect and the applicable statutory conditions are met. The scope and financial effect should be checked against the current legislation and facts.
  • Post-2022 remediation attempts: Under section 120(4)(c), if remedial works carried out after 28 June 2022 fail or cause further safety risks, the costs to rectify those failures are also protected relevant defects.
  • Legal & compliance costs: Certain legal, professional and other costs connected with building-safety defects, enforcement or compliance may be restricted from recovery through the service charge. The precise restriction depends on the statutory provision, the nature of the cost, the lease and the facts.
Scenario Covered by Schedule 8 Protections? Statutory Basis
Defect created 1992–2022, works carried out today YES (Protected / Capped) Building Safety Act 2022, s.120(2) & s.120(4)(a)–(b)
Remedial works carried out after June 2022 that fail YES (Protected / Capped) Building Safety Act 2022, s.120(4)(c)
Landlord legal/professional fees for regulatory compliance NO (Landlord cannot pass on) Building Safety Act 2022, Schedule 8, paragraph 9
Defect created in an original build prior to 28 June 1992 NO (Outside 30-year window) Ordinary leasehold / service charge rules apply
Routine maintenance, wear-and-tear, or cosmetic repairs NO (Not a relevant safety defect) Ordinary Section 20 / service charge rules apply

The fact to establish

Check if your lease is a "qualifying lease" (long lease granted before 14 February 2022, principal residence on that date, or owning no more than 3 UK properties). Ask the landlord for a completed Landlord Certificate confirming developer affiliation and net wealth before paying any major building safety levy.

Law and legislation: Building Safety Act 2022, sections 116–125 and Schedule 8; The Building Safety (Leaseholder Protections) (Information etc.) (England) Regulations 2022 (SI 2022/859).
Who to contact: The freeholder/landlord for the Landlord Certificate; LEASE for free protection checks; or the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber) for a Remediation Order or challenge under Schedule 8.
What is the Leaseholder Deed of Certificate (LHDOC), and what protections can it provide?

What it is

The Leaseholder Deed of Certificate (LHDOC) is the prescribed document used to provide information relevant to whether a lease qualifies for financial protections under Schedule 8 of the Building Safety Act 2022. It records information about the lease, the property and the leaseholder’s circumstances at the statutory qualifying date: 14 February 2022.

Completing the certificate is not an admission of liability or an agreement to pay disputed costs.

What protections can apply?

Where the statutory conditions are met, Schedule 8 may prevent or limit recovery through the service charge of costs relating to certain historical building-safety defects.

  • Cladding: qualifying leaseholders may have no liability for cladding-remediation costs.
  • Landlord or developer responsibility: costs may not be recoverable where the statutory responsibility conditions apply.
  • Lower-value leases: the permitted contribution may be zero where the statutory value threshold applies.
  • Cost caps: permitted non-cladding contributions may be subject to statutory limits.
  • Related professional costs: certain legal and professional costs may not be recoverable through the service charge.

These protections do not automatically apply to every repair, maintenance item, fire-safety measure or service-charge demand. The alleged problem must fall within the statutory definition of a relevant defect, and the other statutory conditions must also be met.

Does the property value matter?

Yes. For a qualifying lease, the value of the lease on 14 February 2022 can affect the permitted contribution towards historical non-cladding building-safety costs.

Where the lease value was below £175,000 outside Greater London, or below £325,000 in Greater London, the permitted contribution may be £0, subject to the statutory conditions.

The relevant figure is not necessarily the price paid by the current leaseholder. It is generally calculated under the statutory rules by reference to the most recent open-market sale and, where necessary, an adjustment using the statutory House Price Index formula.

If the value was above the applicable threshold, other Schedule 8 protections or statutory caps may still apply.

Are apartment entrance doors covered?

Apartment entrance doors are not automatically covered or automatically excluded. A door issue may be relevant to fire safety, but Schedule 8 protection depends on whether the issue meets the statutory definition of a relevant defect and whether the other conditions are satisfied.

Routine repairs, inspections, maintenance, wear and tear or upgrades may fall outside Schedule 8, depending on the facts and the lease. Responsibility for the door is a separate question and should be checked against the lease.

Where a door-related charge is proposed, ask for the alleged defect, the evidence of risk, the date and nature of the relevant building work, the lease clause relied upon and the explanation of why Schedule 8 does or does not apply.

Why does completing the certificate matter?

The certificate provides information needed to assess whether the lease qualifies for the statutory protections. If a valid request is made, the leaseholder should respond within the statutory timeframe and keep a copy of the completed certificate, supporting evidence and delivery records.

Law and legislation: Building Safety Act 2022, sections 116–125 and Schedule 8; The Building Safety (Leaseholder Protections) (Information etc.) (England) Regulations 2022; and GOV.UK guidance on the Leaseholder Deed of Certificate . See also GOV.UK guidance on remediation costs and GOV.UK guidance on the qualifying date and qualifying lease .
What to ask for: If the landlord or managing agent says that a leaseholder is not protected, ask for the statutory basis, the relevant landlord’s certificate, the calculation of any property value used, and an explanation of why the alleged defect or charge falls inside or outside Schedule 8.
Who pays for what defect?

What it is

Not every defect is necessarily recoverable through the service charge. Who ultimately pays can depend on the lease wording, the factual cause of the problem, the contractual chain, insurance or warranty rights, limitation issues, and any statutory protection that applies.

Broad rule of thumb

  • Routine repair, maintenance and wear-and-tear: Often recoverable through the service charge if the lease allows it and the costs were reasonably incurred.
  • Original construction defects or poor refurbishment work: Not automatically recoverable from leaseholders merely because the defect appears in a communal element or the landlord has received an invoice; liability depends on the lease, contractual and statutory rights, insurance, warranties and the facts.
  • Historical building-safety defects: Building Safety Act protections may prevent or cap recovery from qualifying leaseholders.
  • Improvements dressed up as repairs: Whether these are recoverable depends heavily on the lease wording and the true nature of the works.
  • Legal, professional, or enforcement costs: These are only recoverable where the lease and the law permit recovery, and some building-safety related costs are restricted or barred.
Type of issue Who may have to pay Key point to check
Ordinary repair or maintenance Often leaseholders through the service charge Does the lease permit recovery, and were the costs reasonably incurred?
Defect from original construction or conversion Potentially developer, contractor, consultant, insurer, warranty provider, landlord, or partly leaseholders depending on the lease and facts Do not assume the service charge is the correct route without tracing contractual liability and available protections
Historical fire or structural safety defect May be protected or capped for qualifying leaseholders Check Schedule 8, certification, and whether the statutory conditions are met
Improvement rather than repair Depends on the lease wording and what was actually done Many leases distinguish repair from upgrade or improvement
Negligence by contractor or managing agent Potentially the negligent party or its insurer Ask whether any claim or notification has been made before recharging the cost

The fact to establish

Ask the landlord or managing agent to identify in writing: the defect, the alleged cause, when it arose, who they say is legally liable, the lease clause relied on for recovery, and whether any insurance, warranty, contractor, developer, or statutory route has been considered first.

Law and legislation: Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, sections 19 and 27A; Building Safety Act 2022, sections 116–125 and Schedule 8; and the lease itself, which remains the starting point for contractual recoverability.
Who to contact: The landlord or managing agent first for the contractual and legal basis of recovery in writing; then the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber) if payability is disputed; and LEASE for initial guidance.
What are the responsibilities of a Director of a Residents' Management Company (RMC)?

What it is

Directors of a Resident Management Company (RMC) or Right to Manage (RTM) company are officers of a limited company. Even where they are unpaid leaseholder volunteers, they must comply with the Companies Act 2006, the company’s constitution and applicable common-law duties. Their duties are owed primarily to the company, and the precise position depends on the facts and the company’s constitution.

The seven statutory director duties

  1. Act within powers (s.171): Comply with the company's constitution (Articles of Association) and exercise powers only for lawful purposes.
  2. Promote the success of the company (s.172): Act in good faith to promote the success of the company for the benefit of its members as a whole, considering long-term impact and fairness between members.
  3. Exercise independent judgment (s.173): Form genuine independent decisions rather than rubber-stamping proposals from managing agents or single board members.
  4. Exercise reasonable care, skill and diligence (s.174): Act with the diligence expected of a reasonably competent person with the knowledge and experience the director possesses.
  5. Avoid conflicts of interest (s.175): Avoid situations where personal, commercial, or family interests conflict with company duties.
  6. Not accept benefits from third parties (s.176): Refuse gifts, hospitality, commissions, or inducements from contractors, managing agents, or insurers.
  7. Declare interests in transactions (s.177): Fully disclose any personal interest in any proposed company contract before it is agreed.

The fact to establish

Ask to inspect company Articles, formal board minutes, conflict registers, and statutory filings. Members meeting the statutory threshold in Companies Act 2006, section 303 may require the directors to call a general meeting, subject to the prescribed conditions, notice requirements and voting-right calculations.

Law and legislation: Companies Act 2006, sections 171–177 (General duties of directors) and sections 303–305 (Requisitioning general meetings).
Who to contact: RMC Company Secretary; Companies House public registry; LEASE.
What is a Recognised Tenants' Association (RTA) and why does it matter?

What it is

A Recognised Tenants' Association (RTA) is an independent, democratic association of leaseholders that has received formal statutory recognition either by written certificate from the landlord or by an order from the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber).

Statutory powers of an RTA

  • Section 20 contractor nomination: The RTA has the statutory right to nominate a contractor from whom the landlord must try to obtain an estimate during major works consultations.
  • Appointment of a surveyor: The RTA has statutory rights under Housing Act 1996 (s.84) to appoint a qualified surveyor to inspect service-charge accounts, documents, and communal parts.
  • Right to be consulted on managing agents: The landlord must give notice to and consult the RTA on the appointment or change of a managing agent.
  • Collective representation: The RTA secretary can request summaries of accounts and inspect vouchers on behalf of all participating members.

The fact to establish

Recognition depends on the statutory requirements and the evidence submitted to the Tribunal. The association should be able to demonstrate genuine leaseholder membership, an appropriate constitution, proper administration and sufficient support; the precise threshold and criteria should be checked against the current legislation and regulations.

Law and legislation: Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, section 29; The Tenants' Associations (Provisions Relating to Recognition and Provision of Information) (England) Regulations 2018 (SI 2018/1043); Housing Act 1996, section 84 and Schedule 4.
Who to contact: Landlord or RMC board for voluntary recognition; or apply to the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber) for a Certificate of Recognition.
What responsibilities should a paid managing agent perform?

What it is

A paid managing agent is a professional commercial contractor engaged under a written Management Agreement to manage day-to-day estate affairs. While the landlord or RMC remains legally accountable, the agent is paid from service charges to execute professional estate administration competently and lawfully.

Core professional responsibilities

  1. Trust accounts & client monies: Ensure service-charge monies are held and administered in accordance with the applicable statutory trust provisions, the lease and the management agreement. The precise obligations may depend on who receives the money, the capacity in which it is held and the relevant statutory exceptions.
  2. Strict Section 20 compliance: Administer statutory consultation timetables, prepare specifications, obtain competitive quotes, and never instruct qualifying works or bill levies without lawful consultation or tribunal dispensation.
  3. Accounting & budgets: Prepare realistic annual budgets, reconcile audited year-end accounts promptly, and provide prompt access to vouchers under sections 21 and 22.
  4. Statutory health & safety: Commission and log required statutory inspections (Fire Risk Assessments, compartmentation, lift inspections, water hygiene/legionella, electrical testing) and advise the landlord on Building Safety Act duties.
  5. Contractor oversight: Vet contractors for public liability insurance, enforce quality standards, and manage tendering impartially without undisclosed commissions.

The fact to establish

Ask to inspect the formal Management Agreement, schedule of core fees versus menu extras, proof of client money protection/trust banking, and proof of professional indemnity insurance.

Law and legislation: Landlord and Tenant Act 1985; Landlord and Tenant Act 1987, section 42; RICS Service Charge Residential Management Code (Secretary of State-approved code under s.87 Leasehold Reform, Housing and Urban Development Act 1993).
Who to contact: Managing agent senior management; the landlord or RMC board; RICS Regulation (where RICS-regulated); approved redress schemes.
What complaint process exists for my managing agent?

What it is

Property managers carrying out regulated property-management work in England may be required to belong to a government-approved redress scheme, subject to the applicable legislation, exemptions and scheme rules. Where a scheme has jurisdiction, it may provide an independent complaints route and may offer remedies or awards within its published limits.

Typical escalation route

  1. Internal complaint: Submit a formal written Stage 1 complaint to the agent's designated complaints officer, detailing specific failures, dates, and requested remedies.
  2. Final viewpoint: Complete the agent's internal procedure until you receive a final written "viewpoint letter", or after 8 weeks have passed without resolution.
  3. Independent redress: Escalate the complaint to the agent's registered redress scheme within 12 months of the final viewpoint letter.
Law and legislation: Enterprise and Regulatory Reform Act 2013, section 83; The Redress Schemes for Lettings Agency Work and Property Management Work (Requirement to Join etc.) (England) Order 2014 (SI 2014/2621).
Who to contact: The managing agent; The Property Ombudsman (TPO); Property Redress Scheme (PRS).
Can leaseholders take over management from the landlord?

What it is

Under the Right to Manage (RTM), qualifying leaseholders have a statutory right to take over block management functions through a specialised company without having to prove fault, mismanagement, or breach of lease by the landlord.

The fact to establish

Check that the building qualifies (self-contained or part of a building, contains at least two flats, at least two-thirds held by qualifying long leaseholders, and non-residential floor area does not exceed 25%). The RTM company must satisfy the statutory membership and notice requirements before serving a claim notice. The required membership level and eligibility conditions should be checked against the building’s facts and the current Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Act 2002 provisions.

Law and legislation: Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Act 2002, Part 2 (sections 71–113).
Who to contact: LEASE for free RTM guidance; specialist block-management solicitors.
What types of leasehold dispute can the First-tier Tribunal decide?

What it is

The First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber) is the specialist judicial body in England that hears residential leasehold disputes. It is an accessible tribunal designed to resolve legal and financial disagreements without needing high-court litigation costs.

Common statutory application types

  • Service charge payability & reasonableness: Section 27A, Landlord and Tenant Act 1985.
  • Dispensation from consultation: Section 20ZA, Landlord and Tenant Act 1985.
  • Appointment of an independent manager: Section 24, Landlord and Tenant Act 1987 (where a landlord or RMC has failed in its management covenants).
  • Administration charge challenges: Schedule 11, Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Act 2002.
  • Right to Manage eligibility: Section 84, Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Act 2002.
  • Building Safety Act Remediation Orders: Section 123, Building Safety Act 2022.
Law and legislation: Tribunals, Courts and Enforcement Act 2007; Landlord and Tenant Act 1985 & 1987; Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Act 2002; Building Safety Act 2022.
Who to contact: HM Courts & Tribunals Service — First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber); LEASE for guidance.

Part 2: Higher-Risk Buildings (HRB) & Building Safety Regulator (BSR)

Mandatory statutory duties that may apply to qualifying higher-risk residential buildings in England. Whether a building falls within the relevant definition depends on the Building Safety Act 2022, regulations, building characteristics and the applicable statutory regime.

Who is the Principal Accountable Person (PAP) for my building?

What it is

In an occupied higher-risk building, the Principal Accountable Person (PAP) is the accountable person identified under the applicable statutory framework as having responsibility for the relevant structure and exterior. The PAP has specified statutory duties relating to the building’s structure and exterior and to the management of building-safety risks; the precise allocation of duties depends on the Building Safety Act 2022, regulations and the building’s accountable-person structure.

The fact to establish

Ask for the PAP's legal corporate name, company number, registered address, designated Single Point of Contact (SPOC), and official BSR registration reference number. Search the public register to verify status.

Law and legislation: Building Safety Act 2022, sections 72–77; The Higher-Risk Buildings (Descriptions and Supplementary Provisions) Regulations 2023 (SI 2023/275).
Who to contact: The PAP; the managing agent; search the BSR Register of High-Rise Residential Buildings.
What resident-engagement strategy must be in place?

What it is

The PAP of an occupied Higher-Risk Building has a mandatory duty to prepare a written Residents' Engagement Strategy, consult all residents aged 16+ and flat owners on its contents, review it regularly, and act in accordance with it.

The fact to establish

Ask for a copy of the formal strategy document, proof of the date residents were consulted, how resident feedback was considered, and the specific channels available for residents to participate in building safety decisions.

Law and legislation: Building Safety Act 2022, section 91; The Higher-Risk Buildings (Management of Safety Risks etc.) (England) Regulations 2023 (SI 2023/283), Part 2.
Who to contact: The PAP first; then report failure to comply to the Building Safety Regulator (BSR).
What building-safety information must I receive automatically?

What it is

Section 89 provides the framework for regulations concerning the provision of prescribed building-safety information. The precise information, recipients, form and timing depend on the applicable regulations and the building’s statutory circumstances.

The fact to establish

Ask when the statutory information pack was distributed. Check the applicable regulations to establish what information must be provided, to whom, in what form and at what time. Depending on the statutory requirements, this may include information about building-safety risks, emergency arrangements, dutyholder contacts and routes for raising complaints.

Law and legislation: Building Safety Act 2022, section 89; The Higher-Risk Buildings (Keeping and Provision of Information etc.) (England) Regulations 2024 (SI 2024/41), regulation 7 and Schedule 2.
Who to contact: The Principal Accountable Person; escalate non-provision to the Building Safety Regulator.
What building-safety documents can I request?

What it is

The Building Safety Act and supporting regulations provide information rights in relation to prescribed building-safety information. The precise documents, eligible requester, response process and any permitted refusal should be checked against the current legislation and regulations.

The fact to establish

Ask which prescribed documents are available under the applicable statutory information rights, and whether any conditions, exclusions or permitted refusal grounds apply. Depending on the regulations and the circumstances, relevant information may include safety-case, risk-assessment, inspection or occurrence-reporting records.

Law and legislation: Building Safety Act 2022, section 92; The Higher-Risk Buildings (Keeping and Provision of Information etc.) (England) Regulations 2024 (SI 2024/41), regulations 14–18 and Schedule 3.
Who to contact: The PAP or relevant Accountable Person. If refused, escalate to the Building Safety Regulator.
What complaint process must exist for building-safety concerns?

What it is

The applicable building-safety regime may require the PAP to establish, operate and publicise an accessible system for receiving and investigating prescribed building-safety complaints. The scope of the system and the required procedure should be checked against the current regulations.

The fact to establish

Ask for a copy of the formal building-safety complaints procedure, submission address, target response timetables, and confirmation of how complaints are logged in the building's permanent records.

Law and legislation: Building Safety Act 2022, section 93; The Higher-Risk Buildings (Management of Safety Risks etc.) (England) Regulations 2023.
Who to contact: The PAP's complaints handler; then escalate unresolved safety risks directly to the BSR Safety Concerns Portal.
What happens if there is a serious building-safety occurrence?

What it is

Accountable Persons are legally mandated to operate a Mandatory Occurrence Reporting (MOR) framework. A safety occurrence that falls within the statutory description and reporting criteria may require assessment and notification through the mandatory-occurrence reporting regime. Whether notification is required depends on the occurrence, the statutory threshold, the responsible dutyholder and the applicable regulations.

The fact to establish

Ask whether an incident was evaluated against mandatory occurrence reporting thresholds, who conducted the assessment, and whether a formal notification was logged with the BSR within statutory deadlines.

Law and legislation: Building Safety Act 2022, section 87; The Higher-Risk Buildings (Mandatory Occurrence Reporting) (England) Regulations 2023 (SI 2023/284).
Who to contact: Emergency services on 999 for imminent danger; the PAP; and the Building Safety Regulator.
What is the "Golden Thread" of building information?

What it is

The “Golden Thread” is the body of building information that dutyholders must maintain, manage and provide in accordance with the applicable building-safety legislation and regulations. The required format, content, retention, access and transfer arrangements depend on the relevant statutory regime. It is intended to provide reliable, relevant and accessible building information throughout the building’s lifecycle; it should not be described as a legally defined “single source of truth” unless that wording is expressly supported by the applicable guidance or regulations.

Statutory storage and maintenance standards

Under statutory regulations, Golden Thread information must be:

  • Digital: Kept in an electronic format that can be transferred without data loss.
  • Accurate & up-to-date: Regularly reviewed, recording who updated each document and when.
  • Accessible: Presented in plain English and made available to the BSR, emergency services, and residents when requested under statutory rights.
  • Secure: Protected by adequate cybersecurity controls against unauthorised tampering or accidental deletion.

What documents form the Golden Thread

The applicable regulations and guidance specify the categories of building information that must be created, kept, maintained or provided. These may include information relating to key building information, the building’s design and construction, structural and fire-safety arrangements, safety-case materials, occurrence reporting and resident engagement, but the precise scope should be checked against the current statutory requirements.

Law and legislation: Building Safety Act 2022, section 88; The Higher-Risk Buildings (Keeping and Provision of Information etc.) (England) Regulations 2024 (SI 2024/41), Part 2 (Regulation 4) and Schedule 1.
Who to contact: The Principal Accountable Person (who has primary responsibility for maintaining the thread); the Building Safety Regulator if dutyholders fail to maintain statutory safety records.

Primary statutory sources to bookmark: Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, Companies Act 2006, Building Safety Act 2022, Service Charges (Consultation Requirements) (England) Regulations 2003, Building Safety (Leaseholder Protections) (Information etc.) (England) Regulations 2022, and Higher-Risk Buildings (Keeping and Provision of Information etc.) (England) Regulations 2024.
