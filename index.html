Leaseholder Rights

A plain-English guide to questions leaseholders can ask in England, the statute and regulations that back each right, and practical escalation routes for inspection, complaint, redress and tribunal action.

Important: This page gives general legal information for leaseholders in England. It is not legal advice, does not determine liability for any specific building or demand, and should always be read alongside the lease, company constitution, the full statutory text, current regulations, and official guidance.

Part 1: General Leasehold Rights, Consultation & Block Governance

Applies to residential leasehold properties across England, covering service charges, consultation, company directors, managing agents, and the Tribunal.

1. Was proper Section 20 consultation carried out for major works?

What it is

Section 20 consultation protects leaseholders where qualifying works would result in any single leaseholder contributing more than £250, or where a qualifying long-term agreement costs any single leaseholder more than £100 in an accounting year.

What a qualifying-works consultation should look like

  1. Notice of Intention: A written notice describing the proposed works, explaining why they are said to be necessary, inviting written observations and allowing leaseholder contractor nominations. The consultation period must run for at least 30 days.
  2. Notice of Estimates: A written notice prepared after tenders are received. It must make at least two estimates available for inspection (including any estimate from a leaseholder-nominated contractor), summarise previous observations, and allow a further 30-day consultation window.
  3. Notice of Award: Required where the awarded contractor was neither the lowest tender nor a leaseholder nominee. It must explain the reasons for the award and summarise observations within 21 days of entering into the contract.

The fact to establish

Ask for copies of all served notices, exact dates and methods of service, the relevant specifications, tenders received, leaseholder nominations, and the timeline showing that no binding commitment was made before the process lawfully concluded.

Law and legislation: Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, sections 20 and 20ZA; Service Charges (Consultation Requirements) (England) Regulations 2003 (SI 2003/1987).
Who to contact: The landlord or managing agent first. If consultation was breached, consider applying to the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber).
2. What does a valid, unbiased, and lawful Section 20 consultation look like?

What it is

Consultation under Section 20 should not be treated as a superficial tick-box exercise or an announcement of decisions already taken. In practice, a tribunal is likely to look for a real opportunity for leaseholders to understand the proposal, make observations, and have those observations considered before the landlord becomes committed to a course of action.

Common indicators of a fair process

  1. Consultation before commitment: The notices should be served before any contract is awarded or other binding commitment is made.
  2. Coherent explanation of the proposed works: Leaseholders should be told, in clear terms, what works are proposed and why they are said to be required.
  3. Proper treatment of nominations and tenders: Where leaseholders or an RTA nominate a contractor, the landlord should comply with the statutory scheme and assess tenders against the same scope.
  4. Conscientious consideration of observations: Written observations should be considered genuinely and responded to in substance.
  5. Sufficiently clear scope: Leaseholders should be able to understand what they are being consulted about and comment in an informed way.
Likely indicators of a fair consultation Possible warning signs
Proposals consulted on before a contractor is appointed Contractor already chosen or effectively instructed before notices expire
Leaseholder-nominated contractors invited to quote on the same scope Nominated contractors ignored or obstructed without explanation
Clear descriptions and inspection material available Vague descriptions or material that does not match the proposed works
Observations addressed in substance Dismissive responses or no real explanation
Statutory timescales observed Pressure for immediate payment or action before consultation closes

The fact to establish

Ask for the consultation audit trail: the tender documents sent out, proof of any nominated-contractor contact, the tender returns, and the written record showing how observations were considered.

Law and legislation: Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, section 20; Service Charges (Consultation Requirements) (England) Regulations 2003; Daejan Investments Ltd v Benson [2013] UKSC 14. General public-law consultation principles are often associated with R v Brent LBC ex p Gunning (1985) 84 LGR 168 and later approval in R (Moseley) v Haringey LBC [2014] UKSC 56, but those authorities should be used with care outside their own context.
Who to contact: Landlord, Resident Management Company, or managing agent; LEASE; or apply to the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber) to challenge consultation validity.
3. What if Section 20 consultation was not done properly?

What it is

If a landlord or management company fails to comply with the statutory Section 20 consultation requirements, recovery is normally capped at £250 per leaseholder for works (£100 for long-term agreements), unless the First-tier Tribunal grants dispensation.

The fact to establish

Ask whether an application for dispensation under section 20ZA has been submitted. Leaseholders can oppose dispensation by identifying real prejudice, for example loss of the chance to nominate contractors, test the market, or challenge scope and price.

Law and legislation: Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, section 20ZA; Daejan Investments Ltd v Benson [2013] UKSC 14.
Who to contact: The First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber); LEASE for procedural guidance.
4. How do I challenge an unreasonable or non-payable service charge?

What it is

Leaseholders can ask the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber) to decide whether a service charge is payable and whether the amount is reasonable.

The fact to establish

Cross-reference the demand against the lease. The key questions usually are: whether the lease permits recovery of the item at all, whether the costs were reasonably incurred, and whether works or services were provided to a reasonable standard.

Law and legislation: Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, section 19 and section 27A.
Who to contact: Landlord or managing agent via formal written dispute; then the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber).
5. How do I inspect service-charge accounts, invoices and receipts?

What it is

Leaseholders have statutory rights to receive a summary of relevant costs and, following that, to inspect the supporting documents.

The fact to establish

Serve a written section 21 request for a summary. Within six months of receiving it, a further section 22 notice can require facilities to inspect accounts, receipts and supporting documents within the statutory period.

Law and legislation: Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, sections 21, 22 and 25.
Who to contact: Landlord, Resident Management Company, or managing agent; your local housing authority; or the First-tier Tribunal.
6. Can I pay a disputed service charge under protest and still challenge it?

What it is

Usually yes. Payment of a disputed sum does not by itself prevent a later section 27A challenge, and many leaseholders pay under protest to reduce the risk of debt recovery action while preserving their position.

The fact to establish

Pay with a contemporaneous written reservation of rights stating that payment is made without admission that the sum is due, reasonable, or recoverable under the lease.

Example reservation of rights:
“I am paying the sum of £[amount] in respect of demand [invoice number] dated [date]. This payment is made strictly under protest and without admission that the charges are due, reasonable, or contractually recoverable. I reserve all rights to challenge payability and reasonableness under section 27A of the Landlord and Tenant Act 1985 and to seek repayment if appropriate. Please also provide the supporting accounts, vouchers, and tender material relied upon.”
Law and legislation: Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, section 27A(2) and section 27A(3).
Who to contact: Landlord or managing agent; the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber).
7. Am I protected from historical building-safety defect costs? (And does the 27 June 2022 cutoff limit current works?)

What it is

Schedule 8 to the Building Safety Act 2022 can protect qualifying leaseholders from paying service charges for certain historical building-safety defects. Whether the protection is total, capped, or unavailable depends on the lease, the building, the nature of the defect, the landlord's status, and the statutory conditions.

Does the 27 June 2022 date mean current works are unprotected?

Not necessarily. The key issue is usually when the relevant defect arose, not simply when remedial works are being carried out.

  • When the defect was created: Section 120 links a “relevant defect” to works done in the 30-year period ending on 27 June 2022.
  • When remedial works are done: Works carried out now can still fall within the Schedule 8 regime if they address a qualifying historical relevant defect.
  • Failed post-2022 remediation: Section 120(4)(c) can also bring later remedial works within the definition where post-28 June 2022 remediation itself gives rise to a further relevant defect.
  • Legal and professional costs: Schedule 8 paragraph 9 restricts recovery, but the exact scope should be checked carefully, including any statutory exceptions.
Scenario Possible Schedule 8 position Key statutory question
Defect created within the statutory period, works carried out now Potentially protected or capped Is it a relevant defect and is the lease a qualifying lease?
Remedial works after June 2022 that themselves create a further defect Potentially protected or capped Does section 120(4)(c) apply on the facts?
Landlord legal or professional costs connected to relevant-defect liability Often irrecoverable from a qualifying lease, subject to paragraph 9 and its terms What exactly were the costs for, and who incurred them?
Defect outside the statutory window or outside the statutory definition Schedule 8 may not apply Does the cost remain recoverable under the lease and general service-charge law?
Routine maintenance or ordinary wear and tear Usually outside Schedule 8 Is this really a relevant safety defect at all?

The fact to establish

Check whether the flat is held under a qualifying lease and ask the landlord for the relevant certification and the legal basis on which any building-safety costs are said to be recoverable.

Law and legislation: Building Safety Act 2022, sections 116–125 and Schedule 8; The Building Safety (Leaseholder Protections) (Information etc.) (England) Regulations 2022 (SI 2022/859).
Who to contact: The landlord/freeholder for certificates and position statements; LEASE; or the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber) where a Schedule 8 issue is disputed.
8. Who pays for what defect?

What it is

Not every defect can lawfully be passed through the service charge. Who ultimately pays can depend on the lease wording, the factual cause of the problem, the contractual chain, insurance or warranty rights, limitation issues, and any statutory protection that applies.

Broad rule of thumb

  • Routine repair, maintenance and wear-and-tear: Often recoverable through the service charge if the lease allows it and the costs were reasonably incurred.
  • Original construction defects or poor refurbishment work: Not automatically recoverable from leaseholders merely because the defect appears in a communal element or the landlord has received an invoice.
  • Historical building-safety defects: Building Safety Act protections may prevent or cap recovery from qualifying leaseholders.
  • Improvements dressed up as repairs: Whether these are recoverable depends heavily on the lease wording and the true nature of the works.
  • Legal, professional, or enforcement costs: These are only recoverable where the lease and the law permit recovery, and some building-safety related costs are restricted or barred.
Type of issue Who may have to pay Key point to check
Ordinary repair or maintenance Often leaseholders through the service charge Does the lease permit recovery, and were the costs reasonably incurred?
Defect from original construction or conversion Potentially developer, contractor, consultant, insurer, warranty provider, landlord, or partly leaseholders depending on the lease and facts Do not assume the service charge is the correct route without tracing contractual liability and available protections
Historical fire or structural safety defect May be protected or capped for qualifying leaseholders Check Schedule 8, certification, and whether the statutory conditions are met
Improvement rather than repair Depends on the lease wording and what was actually done Many leases distinguish repair from upgrade or improvement
Negligence by contractor or managing agent Potentially the negligent party or its insurer Ask whether any claim or notification has been made before recharging the cost

The fact to establish

Ask the landlord or managing agent to identify in writing: the defect, the alleged cause, when it arose, who they say is legally liable, the lease clause relied on for recovery, and whether any insurance, warranty, contractor, developer, or statutory route has been considered first.

Law and legislation: Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, sections 19 and 27A; Building Safety Act 2022, sections 116–125 and Schedule 8; and the lease itself, which remains the starting point for contractual recoverability.
Who to contact: The landlord or managing agent first for the contractual and legal basis of recovery in writing; then the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber) if payability is disputed; and LEASE for initial guidance.
9. What are the responsibilities of a Director of a Residents' Management Company (RMC)?

What it is

Directors of a Residents' Management Company (RMC) or Right to Manage company are company directors and remain subject to the general duties set out in the Companies Act 2006.

The seven statutory director duties

  1. Act within powers (s.171): Comply with the company's constitution and use powers for proper purposes.
  2. Promote the success of the company (s.172): Act in good faith for the benefit of members as a whole.
  3. Exercise independent judgment (s.173): Avoid simply rubber-stamping third-party recommendations.
  4. Exercise reasonable care, skill and diligence (s.174): Meet the objective and subjective standard expected by the Act.
  5. Avoid conflicts of interest (s.175): Identify and manage conflicts properly.
  6. Not accept benefits from third parties (s.176): Avoid improper inducements.
  7. Declare interests in transactions (s.177): Disclose relevant interests before the company enters the transaction.

The fact to establish

Ask to inspect the Articles, recent minutes, conflict registers, and Companies House filings. Members holding the statutory threshold can also seek to require a general meeting under the Companies Act 2006.

Law and legislation: Companies Act 2006, sections 171–177 and sections 303–305.
Who to contact: Company officers; Companies House; LEASE.
10. What is a Recognised Tenants' Association (RTA) and why does it matter?

What it is

A Recognised Tenants' Association is an association of qualifying tenants that has been recognised by the landlord or by a tribunal certificate.

Why it matters

  • Section 20 nominations: It can nominate contractors under the consultation regime.
  • Collective representation: It can help coordinate observations and document requests.
  • Surveyor rights: Statutory rights may arise in relation to appointing a surveyor under the Housing Act 1996 framework.

The fact to establish

Tribunal recognition depends on the statutory rules and evidence of genuine representation, a constitution, and proper operation.

Law and legislation: Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, section 29; The Tenants' Associations (Provisions Relating to Recognition and Provision of Information) (England) Regulations 2018 (SI 2018/1043); Housing Act 1996, section 84 and Schedule 4.
Who to contact: Landlord or RMC board for voluntary recognition; or the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber) for tribunal recognition.
11. What responsibilities should a paid managing agent perform?

What it is

A paid managing agent is a professional contractor engaged to carry out management functions. The precise scope depends on the management agreement, while the landlord or management company usually remains the principal decision-maker.

Core responsibilities commonly expected

  1. Client money handling: Service-charge monies should be handled in accordance with the legal and regulatory framework.
  2. Consultation administration: Where instructed, the agent should administer statutory consultation properly.
  3. Accounts and budgets: Prepare budgets, accounts and supporting records competently.
  4. Health and safety administration: Arrange required inspections and keep appropriate records.
  5. Contractor management: Procure, monitor and document contractor performance transparently.

The fact to establish

Ask to inspect the formal management agreement, the schedule of fees and extras, the client money arrangements, and the professional indemnity position.

Law and legislation: Landlord and Tenant Act 1985; Landlord and Tenant Act 1987, section 42; RICS Service Charge Residential Management Code (approved code under section 87 of the Leasehold Reform, Housing and Urban Development Act 1993).
Who to contact: Managing agent senior management; the landlord or RMC board; RICS Regulation where applicable; approved redress schemes.
12. What complaint process applies to a managing agent?

What it is

In England, persons engaged in property management work are generally required to belong to an approved redress scheme. That gives leaseholders an escalation route after the agent's internal complaints process has been exhausted.

Typical escalation route

  1. Internal complaint: Send a written complaint identifying the failures, dates, and the remedy sought.
  2. Final response: Complete the internal procedure or wait for the relevant deadline under the agent's process.
  3. Independent redress: Escalate to the scheme to which the agent belongs, within that scheme's rules and timescales.
Law and legislation: Enterprise and Regulatory Reform Act 2013, section 83; The Redress Schemes for Lettings Agency Work and Property Management Work (Requirement to Join etc.) (England) Order 2014 (SI 2014/2621).
Who to contact: The managing agent; The Property Ombudsman (TPO); Property Redress Scheme (PRS).
13. Can leaseholders take over management from the landlord?

What it is

Under the Right to Manage, qualifying leaseholders can acquire management functions through an RTM company without proving fault by the landlord.

The fact to establish

Check the statutory qualification criteria for the building and participation thresholds before any claim notice is served.

Law and legislation: Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Act 2002, Part 2 (sections 71–113).
Who to contact: LEASE for RTM guidance; specialist advisers where needed.
14. What types of leasehold dispute can the First-tier Tribunal decide?

What it is

The First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber) is the main specialist forum in England for many residential leasehold disputes.

Common statutory application types

  • Service charge payability and reasonableness: Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, section 27A.
  • Dispensation from consultation: Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, section 20ZA.
  • Appointment of a manager: Landlord and Tenant Act 1987, section 24.
  • Administration charges: Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Act 2002, Schedule 11.
  • Right to Manage disputes: Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Act 2002.
  • Building Safety Act remediation applications: Building Safety Act 2022.
Law and legislation: Tribunals, Courts and Enforcement Act 2007; Landlord and Tenant Act 1985 and 1987; Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Act 2002; Building Safety Act 2022.
Who to contact: HM Courts & Tribunals Service — First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber); LEASE.

Part 2: Higher-Risk Buildings (HRB) & Building Safety Regulator (BSR)

Mandatory statutory duties applying to residential buildings in England that meet the Higher-Risk Building definition.

15. Who is the Principal Accountable Person (PAP) for my building?

What it is

In an occupied Higher-Risk Building, the Principal Accountable Person is the person with the relevant legal estate in possession in the common parts or, if there is more than one Accountable Person, the one responsible for the structure and exterior.

The fact to establish

Ask for the PAP's legal identity, contact details, and the building's registration status.

Law and legislation: Building Safety Act 2022, sections 72–77; The Higher-Risk Buildings (Descriptions and Supplementary Provisions) Regulations 2023 (SI 2023/275).
Who to contact: The PAP; the managing agent; or the BSR Register of High-Rise Residential Buildings.
16. What resident-engagement strategy must be in place?

What it is

The Principal Accountable Person must prepare, review, and act in accordance with a resident engagement strategy for occupied higher-risk buildings, following the statutory framework.

The fact to establish

Ask for the strategy, when it was last reviewed, and how residents can participate in building-safety decisions.

Law and legislation: Building Safety Act 2022, section 91; The Higher-Risk Buildings (Management of Safety Risks etc.) (England) Regulations 2023 (SI 2023/283), Part 2.
Who to contact: The PAP first; then the Building Safety Regulator.
17. What building-safety information must I receive automatically?

What it is

The 2024 information regulations require certain prescribed information about the building and its safety management to be provided proactively to residents and owners. Exactly what must be provided, and to whom, depends on the regulations.

The fact to establish

Ask when the prescribed information was last issued and which document or notice was used to provide it.

Law and legislation: Building Safety Act 2022, section 89; The Higher-Risk Buildings (Keeping and Provision of Information etc.) (England) Regulations 2024 (SI 2024/41), including regulation 7 and Schedule 2.
Who to contact: The Principal Accountable Person; escalate significant non-compliance to the Building Safety Regulator.
18. What building-safety documents can I request?

What it is

Section 92 and the 2024 regulations give residents and owners rights to request prescribed information and documents, but the exact categories, exclusions, and routes of access are defined by the regulations rather than by a single open-ended right to every safety document.

The fact to establish

Make the request in writing and identify the category of information sought. Check the regulations carefully before assuming that a particular report, assessment, or log must be provided in full.

Law and legislation: Building Safety Act 2022, section 92; The Higher-Risk Buildings (Keeping and Provision of Information etc.) (England) Regulations 2024 (SI 2024/41), regulations 14–18 and Schedule 3.
Who to contact: The PAP or relevant Accountable Person; then the Building Safety Regulator where appropriate.
19. What complaint process must exist for building-safety concerns?

What it is

The Principal Accountable Person must operate a complaints system for prescribed building-safety complaints under the statutory framework.

The fact to establish

Ask for the written complaints procedure, where complaints should be sent, and how the complaint will be recorded and escalated.

Law and legislation: Building Safety Act 2022, section 93; The Higher-Risk Buildings (Management of Safety Risks etc.) (England) Regulations 2023.
Who to contact: The PAP's complaints contact; then the BSR Safety Concerns Portal for serious unresolved safety issues.
20. What happens if there is a serious building-safety occurrence?

What it is

There is a mandatory occurrence reporting regime for certain safety events in occupied higher-risk buildings.

The fact to establish

Ask whether the event was assessed against the reporting criteria, who made that assessment, and whether notification was given where required.

Law and legislation: Building Safety Act 2022, section 87; The Higher-Risk Buildings (Mandatory Occurrence Reporting) (England) Regulations 2023 (SI 2023/284).
Who to contact: Emergency services for immediate danger; the PAP; and the Building Safety Regulator where the reporting regime is engaged.
21. What is the "Golden Thread" of building information?

What it is

The Golden Thread is the statutory body of information that must be kept and maintained for higher-risk buildings under the post-Building Safety Act regime.

Core characteristics

  • Digital: Kept in an appropriate electronic form.
  • Accurate and up to date: Maintained as the building and its safety case evolve.
  • Accessible: Available to the persons and bodies entitled to it under the regime.
  • Secure: Managed to reduce the risk of loss or unauthorised alteration.

What documents may form part of it

The detailed contents are set by the regulations and can include key building information, safety case material, and other prescribed records.

Law and legislation: Building Safety Act 2022, section 88; The Higher-Risk Buildings (Keeping and Provision of Information etc.) (England) Regulations 2024 (SI 2024/41), regulation 4 and Schedule 1.
Who to contact: The Principal Accountable Person; or the Building Safety Regulator where statutory record-keeping appears deficient.

Primary statutory sources to bookmark: Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, Companies Act 2006, Building Safety Act 2022, Service Charges (Consultation Requirements) (England) Regulations 2003, Building Safety (Leaseholder Protections) (Information etc.) (England) Regulations 2022, and Higher-Risk Buildings (Keeping and Provision of Information etc.) (England) Regulations 2024.
